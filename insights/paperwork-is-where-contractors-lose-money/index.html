You Won't Lose Money on Site. You'll Lose It in the Paperwork.

Singapore has more construction work than it has had in a decade. The firms that get paid on time will be the ones that fix the back office first.

22 November 2026 · 6-minute read

Singapore's construction industry just had its best year in a decade. BCA puts 2025 construction demand at S$50.5 billion, and expects S$47 to 53 billion in 2026, carried by Changi Terminal 5, new rail lines and public housing. Output this year is forecast to rise about 7%.

For most contractors, that means more jobs running at once with the same people. And when the team is stretched, the first thing to slip is not the concrete pour. It is the paperwork that turns finished work into cash, and the paperwork that proves the site was run safely.

Finished work is not money yet

Every month, a contractor turns site progress into a payment claim. Under the Security of Payment Act, the clock then starts. The paying party has until the date in the contract, and never more than 21 days, to issue a payment response. If the contract is silent, it is 14 days.

What the response says depends almost entirely on what the claim shows. A claim that cannot be traced to the contract's bill of quantities, or that lists a variation with no written instruction behind it, gets cut. The work was done. The evidence was not attached.

Timing is just as unforgiving. Many contracts set a monthly cut-off, and a claim that misses it is treated as next month's claim. One late evening, one missing site record, and a month of cash moves back by a month.

Where the hours go

In most contracting firms, the claim is built the same way. The QS or PM collects site diary entries, photos from WhatsApp groups, delivery orders, subcontractor invoices and variation emails. Then they rebuild the month in a spreadsheet, line by line, against the contract. It is careful work. It is also mostly copying, matching and chasing.

The safety file grows the same way. Risk assessments need updating when the work changes. Permits to work, toolbox meetings and inspection checklists all need records. These documents matter: they are how a contractor shows the site was run properly when something goes wrong.

That matters more on smaller jobs. MOM reports that small-scale construction works, such as additions, alterations and renovation, made up 70% of the sector's fatal and major injuries in the first half of 2026. MOM says it is reviewing ways to strengthen accountability among the contractors who do them.

So the people who should be walking the site are filling in forms at 10pm instead.

What AI should do here, and what it should not

This is exactly the kind of work AI is now good at: reading scattered records, matching them to a structure, and producing a first draft that a qualified person checks.

For a progress claim, that means:

  • Pulling the month's site records, photos and delivery orders into one place.
  • Mapping completed work to the contract's line items and flagging anything that does not match.
  • Listing variations that have no written instruction yet, before the claim goes out.
  • Producing a claim draft, with supporting documents attached, days before the cut-off.

For the safety file, it means drafting risk assessment updates when the work scope changes, and keeping permit and toolbox records complete and dated.

What AI should not do is sign anything. The QS still certifies the numbers. The PM still approves the claim. The safety officer still owns the risk assessment. AI drafts; your people decide.

Start with one process, and measure it

You do not need a platform or a new system to test this. Pick the one process that costs your team the most evenings. For most contractors, that is the monthly progress claim.

Run it for two or three claim cycles and track three numbers:

  1. Days between your cut-off and the day the claim is served.
  2. The share of each claim that is certified, and the reasons for any cuts.
  3. Hours your QS or PM spends on the claim.

If those numbers move, you have your answer. If they do not, you have spent a few weeks, not a year.

There is also help with the cost. The enhanced Productivity Solutions Grant, from April 2026, adds more pre-approved digital solutions for built environment firms, including digital contract management. Check whether the solution you choose is on the list before you sign.

The question for 2026

Demand is not the problem this year. Capacity is. Every contractor will be asked to do more with the same team. The ones who get paid on time will not be the ones with the fastest crews. They will be the ones whose claims are complete, on time, and hard to argue with.
